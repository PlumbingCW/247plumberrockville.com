Installing a Basement Sump Pump in Rockville

A sump pump sits in a pit at the lowest point of a basement and pushes groundwater outside before it reaches the floor. This guide explains how a new installation is laid out and what to decide first.

How a sump system works

Groundwater collects in a pit about two feet deep. A float switch rises with the water and turns the pump on. The pump moves water up a discharge pipe, through a check valve that stops backflow and out of the house. When the level drops, the pump shuts off.

The pit is often connected to drain pipe laid around the footing, which gathers water from the perimeter. If you are weighing a bigger project, see our notes on basement waterproofing, and for a pump that has already failed, sump pump repair.

SubmersibleQuiet, handles solids, sits in the pitHarder to reach, shorter service access.

Planning the pit and discharge

  • Place the pit at the lowest point and make it level.
  • Use a sealed lid to keep humidity and radon out of the room.
  • Add a check valve on the discharge to stop water from running back.
  • Run the discharge outdoors and away from the foundation, with a path that will not freeze solid.

Discharge is where many installs go wrong. If water returns to the foundation, the pump simply recycles it. A frozen or kinked outlet line backs water into the pit. A good installer routes it with a steady downhill pitch and a spot to release even if the first outlet ices over.

Mistakes that make a new sump pump fail

  • Placing the pit too shallow, so the pump short cycles.
  • Skipping the check valve, so water runs back into the pit.
  • Setting the float where it can catch on the pit wall.
  • Using a discharge pipe that is too small or freezes shut.
  • Forgetting a backup for power failures.

Each one is invisible on a dry day and obvious in a storm. A pump that short cycles burns out early, a missing check valve doubles the work and a blocked discharge floods the pit. Testing the finished system with a bucket of water is the simple way to find them before the rain does.

If your existing pump runs constantly, do not assume it needs replacing. A stuck float or a failed check valve can mimic a bad pump, and either is a cheap fix.

Cost factors to expect

The price of a sump pump installation depends on whether a pit already exists, how far the discharge must run, the pump type and whether a backup is added. Breaking concrete to set a new pit adds labor, and a long discharge line adds material. Ask for a written scope that lists the pit, the pump, the check valve, the lid and the discharge, so you can compare quotes fairly.

Sizing and backup

Most homes do fine with a one-third to one-half horsepower pump. A house with a high water table or a lot of runoff may need more capacity. What counts more than size is reliability, because a pump that fails in a storm costs more than one that was sized up.

A battery backup is cheap insurance for homes where storms often cut the power. It also pays to test the pump yearly by pouring a bucket of water into the pit, and to replace a pump that is approaching a decade of service.

Where the water is allowed to go

Storm water and ground water are among the discharges WSSC Water prohibits in the sanitary sewer, which is why a sump pump should discharge outdoors. Montgomery County lists plumbing permits under WSSC, and advises residents who live in a municipality or HOA to check local rules as well.

Rockville is a municipality, so ask the city and your HOA about discharge location and any lot requirements, and ask WSSC about permits for the plumbing itself. We confirm which rules apply before we start.
