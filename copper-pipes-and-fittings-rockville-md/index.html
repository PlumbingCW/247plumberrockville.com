Copper Pipes and Fittings for Rockville Homes

Copper has carried drinking water through American houses for generations, and many Rockville homes still rely on it. This guide covers the pipe types, the common fittings and the failure signs worth knowing.

The three common copper pipe types

Copper water tube comes in types K, L and M, and the difference is wall thickness. Type K is the thickest and is used for underground service. Type L is the usual choice for interior supply lines. Type M is the thinnest and is common in older homes where cost mattered.

For a homeowner the point is simple. A thicker wall tolerates more corrosion before it develops a pinhole, so the type matters when you are choosing a repair. A plumber can read the stamp or color band printed on the pipe: green for K, blue for L and red for M.

Fittings you will see on a copper system

Fittings are the elbows, tees, couplings and valves that connect straight pipe. Most copper is joined by soldering, where a fitting is heated and filled with filler metal. Press fittings use a tool to crimp a sealed joint without flame, and compression fittings use a nut and sleeve, which suit repairs and valve connections.

Each has a place. Soldered joints are the traditional standard. Press fittings help where an open flame is risky, such as near wood framing. Compression fittings are useful for fixture supply lines but need to be tight and accessible, since they are the ones that weep first.

How copper pipe fails

  • Pinhole leaks appear when water or flux erodes thin spots in the wall.
  • Joint leaks come from poor solder, vibration or movement over the years.
  • Freeze splits open a lengthwise crack in a pipe that was not insulated.
  • Dezincification attacks brass fittings, which turn pink and brittle.

One pinhole is a repair. A pattern of pinholes in different rooms points to pipe wall failure and is the moment to ask about repiping. If you cannot tell whether a stain is new, a quick check with leak detection avoids opening walls blindly.

Signs your copper needs attention

Copper rarely fails without warning. Green or blue crust at joints, white mineral scale around fittings, tiny pinhole sprays and a pipe that feels damp when the rest is dry all point to trouble. Water that tastes metallic or looks blue-green after sitting can also signal corrosion.

Catching these early is cheaper than waiting for a flood. A plumber can pressure test the system, check for pin leaks at joints and tell you whether a few repairs will do or whether the system as a whole is near its limit. If your supply lines are old, ask about the condition of shutoff valves, since those fail first.

  • Check exposed pipes in the basement twice a year for crust or damp spots.
  • Listen for ticking or banging, which can mean loose pipe or pressure problems.
  • Look at the water meter with everything off for slow movement.
  • Keep records of repairs so patterns become clear.

Rockville water and your copper pipes

Copper corrodes depending on water chemistry, so it helps to know your supply. The City of Rockville treats and distributes its own water from the Potomac River for roughly 70 percent of the city, and WSSC Water serves the rest. The city's 2025 water quality report lists lead at under 1 part per billion at the 90th percentile.

The report does not list a hardness figure, so we do not give one. If you want to know how your water treats copper and fixtures, ask your utility or test your tap. For plumbing work itself, WSSC Water is the plumbing inspection agency for Montgomery County and has its own code, so ask before a larger job which permit rules apply.
