Epoxy Lining for Sewer Pipes in Rockville

Epoxy lining, often called cured-in-place pipe, repairs a damaged sewer by coating the inside of the old pipe with a resin sleeve. It avoids a long trench, but it does not suit every pipe.

If sewage is backing up now, stop water use and call a plumber first. Lining is a planned repair.

How the lining process works

The crew cleans the line and inspects it with a camera. A felt or fiberglass sleeve soaked in resin is pulled or inverted into the pipe, then inflated against the walls. Heat, steam or ultraviolet light cures the resin, and a hard new pipe forms inside the old one.

The result seals cracks and joints and blocks roots from entering. It leaves the existing pipe in place, so it can reduce digging up a lawn or driveway. A camera pass first is essential, which is why our sewer inspection page is where most conversations begin.

What lining cannot fix

Lining is a good tool in the right place. It cannot repair a pipe that has collapsed, because the liner cannot pass through. It cannot level a belly, where water stands in a sag, because the liner follows the shape of the old pipe. It is also not a substitute for connections that lead into the line, which can leak at the joint with the liner.

Reinstating side connections is a step that crews handle by cutting openings after the liner cures. Ask how that is done, and whether the work will be checked with a camera at the end. A good installer shares the footage before and after, so you can see what changed.

  • Ask for a before and after video.
  • Ask how branch lines are reconnected.
  • Confirm the liner material and its cure method.
  • Get the warranty terms in writing.

Costs and what drives them

Lining costs depend on pipe length, diameter, the number of access points and whether cleaning or root removal is needed first. Excavating at both ends for access also adds to the bill, so a lining job is cheaper when cleanouts exist. We do not quote a price in a guide like this because it depends on your pipe, and a camera inspection comes first.

Compare quotes by scope, not by headline. A lower price that skips cleaning, skips the final camera pass or leaves out the branch reconnections is not a better deal. Ask each contractor to list every step in writing.

Signs a lined pipe needs a second look

Lined pipes should run quietly and drain freely. If you see new backups, a smell or slow drains within the first months, tell the installer and ask for a camera check. A liner that did not bond, a branch connection that was not reopened or a bend that was missed can show up early, and the warranty is there for that.

Lining compared with digging

Open-trench replacement gives you a brand-new pipe along a known path. It also means excavation, restoration and often the longest disruption. Lining trades that for a thinner new wall and the need for an intact path. For a broken pipe, repair choices are laid out on our sewer line repair page.

Questions to ask before you agree

  • Can I see the camera footage that shows the lining is suitable?
  • Who holds the required permit and registration for the work?
  • How will the line be cleaned and prepared first?
  • What is covered if the liner fails?

Permits and registration for underground sewer work

In WSSC Water territory, only a registered WSSC Master Plumber may excavate and repair underground sewer pipes, and the work needs a WSSC permit and inspection. WSSC also says the owner is responsible for the pipe from the property line to the building.

Ask any contractor, ours included, for their registration number. WSSC Water is the right place to confirm whether a lining job needs a permit, and we do not guess at rules.
