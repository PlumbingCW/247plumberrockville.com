Bathroom Sink Plumbing Installation in Rockville

A bathroom sink looks simple, but behind the vanity are supply lines, shutoff valves, a trap and a vent. Getting each one right is what separates a dry cabinet from a slow leak.

If a sink is leaking right now, close the two small valves under it and call a plumber.

What lives under the vanity

Two supply lines carry hot and cold water from small shutoff valves, called stops, to the faucet. A pop-up drain assembly connects the basin to a curved trap. The trap holds a plug of water that blocks sewer gas, and it connects to the drain pipe in the wall, which is vented so water flows without gurgling.

Old shutoff valves are the first thing to replace on a remodel. If they have not been turned in years, they can fail the moment you touch them. Fixing a faucet instead? See our faucet repair page.

The installation sequence

  1. Close the stops and open the faucet to drain the lines.
  2. Disconnect the old supply lines, the trap and the old drain assembly.
  3. Set the new faucet on the sink before mounting it, which is much easier than working under the cabinet.
  4. Install the drain assembly with plumber's putty or a gasket as the manufacturer instructs.
  5. Mount the sink, connect the supply lines hand tight plus a small turn, and rebuild the trap.
  6. Run water for several minutes and check every joint with a dry tissue.

Choosing parts that last

Fittings vary in quality more than most people realize. Metal supply lines with braided covers last longer than thin plastic ones, brass shutoff valves outlast cheap alloys and a trap made of heavy-gauge material stands up to bumping. The savings from low-cost parts are small compared with the cost of a leak inside a vanity.

Match the drain to the sink. A pop-up drain assembly has an overflow path that has to line up with the sink's overflow hole, and a vessel sink needs a different drain from a standard one. Check the specifications before you buy, and keep the paperwork in case a part needs replacing.

  • Use braided stainless supply lines with new compression fittings.
  • Replace old shutoff valves with quarter-turn ball valves.
  • Choose a trap with a clean-out nut for easier service.
  • Test with the cabinet empty so any drip is easy to see.

When to call a plumber instead

Swapping a faucet is a reasonable weekend job. Moving a sink, changing the drain height or adding a second basin is different, because the drain and vent have to work together. A drain that is too high or a vent that is missing will gurgle, siphon the trap dry and let sewer gas into the room.

Call a plumber if the shutoff valves will not close, if you find corrosion on the wall pipes or if the wall is damp. Those are signs of a hidden problem, and a quick visit can prevent a bigger one.

Checking your work after a week

A sink that is dry on day one can still weep slowly. Check under the vanity again after a day, a week and a month. Touch every joint with a dry tissue, since a faint dampness shows up better than a glance. Slow leaks swell cabinet floors and loosen fittings, so tighten a nut a fraction of a turn only if you see moisture.

Mistakes that cause leaks

  • Overtightening plastic nuts, which cracks them.
  • Reusing old washers or rubber gaskets.
  • A trap that is not aligned with the wall drain, which strains the joint.
  • Skipping a test, then finding a drip a week later.

If the wall behind the vanity is damp or the drain gurgles, the issue may be the vent or the wall drain, which is bigger than a sink swap. In that case, plumbing installation services cover rough-in work.

Permits and water quality

Montgomery County's permit guidance lists plumbing permits under WSSC Water, the plumbing inspection agency with its own code. A like-for-like sink swap usually involves less than a new drain or vent, but ask WSSC before moving drains.

Rockville's 2025 water quality report lists lead at under 1 part per billion at the 90th percentile and no hardness figure, so ask your utility or test your tap if you want to choose a faucet finish with scale in mind.
