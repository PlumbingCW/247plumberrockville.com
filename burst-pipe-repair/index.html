Burst pipe: what to do first

Speed matters more than anything else. Find the main shutoff, usually where the water line enters the house near the meter or in the basement, and turn it clockwise until it stops. If you cannot find it, call us and we will talk you through it.

Then protect what you can. Lift furniture, move valuables, and run a mop or wet vac only where there is no electrical risk. A related question is how to find a leak that is not a burst, which we cover on our leak detection page.

Steps while you wait

  • Close the main water shutoff.
  • Turn off the water heater to avoid damaging it if the tank drains.
  • Switch off breakers to flooded rooms only if you can reach the panel dry.
  • Photograph the damage and the pipe before cleanup for your insurer.

Why pipes burst and how we repair them

Most bursts in this area come from freezing. Water expands as it turns to ice, and the pressure splits the pipe at its weakest point, often in an exterior wall, a crawl space or an unheated garage. Other causes are corrosion at old fittings, high water pressure and a failed joint.

After the water is stopped

Once the main is closed and the pipe is repaired, the water still has to go somewhere. Remove standing water, run fans and a dehumidifier, and lift wet carpet and padding quickly, since mold can start within a couple of days.

Take photos before cleanup and keep receipts for emergency measures. If drywall or insulation is soaked, an insurer will usually want to see it documented, and a plumber's written note on the cause helps your claim.

Freezing risk in Rockville homes

Winter cold snaps catch pipes in exterior walls and unheated spaces, particularly where insulation is thin. To thaw a frozen pipe, open the faucet, warm the pipe with a hair dryer and never use an open flame. If the pipe has already split, the water will come back on once it thaws.

Prevention is cheap. Insulate exposed pipes, let a faucet drip during a hard freeze and keep cabinet doors open on exterior walls. For a high-pressure problem, a pressure reducing valve is worth discussing, and our Potomac plumbing page covers larger homes with long supply runs.
